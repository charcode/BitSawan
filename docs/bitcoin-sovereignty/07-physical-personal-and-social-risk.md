# Physical, Personal, and Social Risk

[← Back to the framework](README.md)

## Objective

Protect life and autonomy from robbery, coercion, manipulation, careless disclosure, relationship conflict, incapacity, impulsive behaviour, and the gradual decline of memory or technical competence.

The holder is part of the security system and will change over time. A plan that requires permanent perfect judgement, secrecy, health, and memory is not resilient.

## Risk register

| Scenario | Prevent | Detect | Response |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Targeted robbery/coercion | Discretion; limited immediately accessible amount; distributed authority | Threats, stalking, data/vendor leak | Prioritise life; use prewritten emergency contacts and safe location |
| Opportunistic discovery | Concealed and access-controlled recovery components | Broken seal, moved item, unexplained access | Treat as possible copy; migrate if spend authority exposed |
| Social engineering | Authentication rules; no seed disclosure; cooling-off period | Urgency, secrecy, impersonation | Stop communication; verify independently |
| Trusted-person abuse | Least authority; threshold separation; role clarity | Boundary violations or pressure | Revoke non-key access; migrate policy if key trust affected |
| Relationship breakdown | Estate/cosigner plan independent of goodwill | Separation, dispute, refusal | Activate written replacement/legal plan early |
| Cognitive decline/incapacity | Periodic competence review; trusted checks; continuity plan | Repeated errors, confusion, missed procedures | Pause unilateral large spends; activate designated assistance |
| Impulsive trading/scam | Layer separation; withdrawal delay; pre-commitment | Rule-breaking, secrecy, obsession | Cooling-off; block access routes; consult trusted person |
| Public disclosure | Minimise balances/architecture discussed publicly | Doxxing or targeted approaches | Reduce actionable exposure; review home/travel plan |

## Disclosure protocol

Apply need-to-know separation:

- public audience may know general Bitcoin interest, not holdings or custody;
- a technical helper may know wallet software/policy but not locations or sufficient secrets;
- a storage host may know an item exists but not what other components complete it;
- an heir may know where instructions are without having immediate unilateral authority;
- an adviser may know values and records without holding keys.

Avoid casual photographs of signing devices, backup products, safes, addresses, fingerprints, home interiors, or travel patterns. Vendor purchase records can connect identity and physical address to Bitcoin ownership; use lawful delivery and data-minimisation choices appropriate to the threat.

## Physical custody protocol

1. Identify who can enter each home, office, storage site, and safe.
2. Separate signer, seed, passphrase, and multisig components according to the required failure tolerance.
3. Use durable containers and tamper evidence appropriate to the environment.
4. Keep access methods from being the same single point of failure as the item protected.
5. Do not routinely transport deep-reserve secrets together.
6. Maintain a non-secret inventory and periodic presence check.
7. Avoid elaborate concealment that heirs cannot identify or that may be discarded during moving/clearance.
8. Reassess after moving home, renovation, staff changes, relationship changes, or public exposure.

## Social-engineering authentication protocol

Before discussing funds or acting on an urgent request:

1. Stop and move to a known communication channel.
2. Authenticate the person using a pre-agreed second method.
3. State the request back, including destination, amount, purpose, and deadline.
4. Refuse remote-control software and refuse all requests for seed words or passphrases.
5. Verify addresses independently on the signing device.
6. For material amounts, require a cooling-off period or second trusted review.
7. Treat secrecy (“do not tell your bank/family”) and urgency as attack indicators.

## Coercion and personal-safety protocol

The first rule is that no amount of bitcoin is worth serious injury or death.

Before any incident, obtain location-appropriate professional advice if the threat is credible. Define safe places, emergency contacts, family communication, and what limited funds are immediately accessible. Distributed signing can reduce one person's unilateral ability to comply, but it can also prolong danger if an attacker expects instant access; do not adopt it as a theatrical defence without considering real human consequences.

During an active threat:

1. Prioritise survival and de-escalation.
2. Do not attempt a technical trick that increases physical danger.
3. Follow local emergency guidance and the prewritten personal plan.
4. Once safe, contact authorities/professionals as appropriate, protect remaining components, and treat disclosed keys as compromised.

This document does not prescribe decoy wallets, booby traps, or resistance tactics. Their safety depends on circumstances and attacker expectations.

## Capacity and self-governance protocol

### Preventive governance

- Separate long-term reserve from trading and spending applications.
- Require a second human check or time delay for transfers above a personal threshold.
- Write rules for leverage, lending, gifts, and speculative assets.
- Keep a trusted person who can notice major behavioural or cognitive change but cannot steal unilaterally.
- Make the continuity plan activate through defined evidence and appropriate legal authority.

### Warning indicators

- repeated passphrase/address confusion;
- missing appointments or inability to explain the wallet;
- secret borrowing or urgent speculative transfers;
- sudden new “advisers” or online relationships;
- bypassing established checks;
- signing while intoxicated, sleep-deprived, distressed, or medically impaired.

### Response

Pause non-essential large transactions. Preserve ordinary living access. Bring in the predesignated trusted/legal/medical support appropriate to the concern. Do not let a vague claim of incapacity become an informal route for another person to seize control.

## Incident playbooks

### Suspicious approach or doxxing

1. Preserve messages and evidence; do not engage provocatively.
2. Remove additional public details and tighten account privacy.
3. Review leaked home, travel, family, vendor, and wallet information.
4. Move people to safety before moving keys if danger is immediate.
5. Obtain professional security/law-enforcement advice appropriate to the threat.

### Trusted person or cosigner becomes unreliable

1. Determine what information and signing authority they possess.
2. Protect remaining components and stop sharing new wallet metadata.
3. If the signing policy's safety margin is affected, create and test a new policy and migrate.
4. Update inheritance and legal documents; do not assume technical migration resolves legal rights.

### Holder becomes temporarily incapacitated

1. Use existing ordinary funds for care rather than exposing deep reserves casually.
2. Activate only the authority defined in the continuity plan.
3. Require documented actions and independent verification for material spends.
4. Restore full control or migrate policy after capacity returns if secrets were exposed.

## Validation tests

- No single casual acquaintance knows holdings, full architecture, and locations.
- A physical-access inventory is current.
- A credible trusted-person failure does not automatically enable theft or permanent loss.
- Emergency contacts and safe-location plans are available without exposing seeds.
- The holder has a threshold above which a second check/cooling-off period applies.
- The incapacity mechanism has both safeguards and a practical activation route.

## Residual risks

Discretion cannot reverse data already disclosed. Geographic distribution may protect keys while increasing travel and coercion complexity. Trusted people reduce individual failure but introduce their own motives, health, and legal exposure. Human safety decisions cannot be reduced to a wallet configuration.
