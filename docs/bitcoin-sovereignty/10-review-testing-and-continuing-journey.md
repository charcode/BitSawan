# Review, Testing, and the Continuing Journey

[← Back to the framework](README.md)

## Objective

Keep the system understandable, supported, recoverable, and proportionate as devices age, software changes, services disappear, people move, relationships evolve, health changes, and the value at risk grows.

Security decays unless maintained. Maintenance, however, can itself create risk. The objective is disciplined change—not constant novelty.

## Review triggers

| Trigger | Required review |
| --- | --- |
| Large increase/decrease in value | Fund layers, concentration, physical threat, inheritance |
| New home, country, or travel pattern | Locations, jurisdiction, access time, border exposure |
| Marriage, separation, birth, death, or dispute | Beneficiaries, trusted people, cosigners, legal documents |
| New wallet/signer/node | Compatibility, policy, recovery, privacy, software provenance |
| Vulnerability or lost component | Affected keys/policy, migration need, correlated exposure |
| Failed or confusing transaction | Procedure, training, labels, device/coordinator behaviour |
| Health/capacity change | Assistance thresholds and continuity activation |
| Exchange/bank restriction | Liquidity and counterparty routes |
| Long inactivity | Firmware/software support, backup condition, skill rehearsal |

## Maintenance calendar

### Monthly or operationally

- reconcile material receipts and spends;
- label new UTXOs and preserve records;
- review account/device security alerts;
- confirm no critical balance remains on an exchange beyond its intended purpose.

### Quarterly

- confirm node/wallet endpoints and failover behaviour;
- check authoritative advisories for signers and wallet software;
- verify backup inventory/tamper evidence without exposing contents;
- review emergency liquidity and known obligations.

### Annually

- repeat the threat model and residual-risk acceptance;
- inspect physical backup condition and locations;
- conduct a controlled recovery/tabletop drill;
- execute a small receive-and-spend rehearsal;
- review trusted people, beneficiaries, legal jurisdictions, and documents;
- verify software/device support and migration readiness;
- update versioned instructions and destroy superseded copies appropriately.

Frequency should rise with transaction activity, change, and credible threat—not merely market price.

## Software and firmware update protocol

1. Do not update during an urgent transaction unless the current version is itself unsafe.
2. Read the vendor/project's authenticated release notes and security advisory.
3. Confirm model/version compatibility and whether the update changes wallet formats, policies, or backup assumptions.
4. Verify download signatures or reproducible authenticity by the project's documented method where available.
5. Confirm recovery information and a compatible fallback before updating.
6. Test on a non-critical device or small-value wallet when practical.
7. After update, verify wallet fingerprint/policy, receive address, and a small signing path.
8. Record version and test date.

An update fixes software; it does not retroactively repair a seed that may have been generated by a faulty random-number process. In that case, fresh entropy and wallet migration may be required.

## Migration protocol

Use for a compromised key, obsolete signer, architecture change, script upgrade, inheritance redesign, or privacy separation.

### Plan

1. State the reason, urgency, assets affected, and threat assumptions.
2. Choose the destination architecture and document its new failure modes.
3. Decide whether the move should consolidate or preserve UTXO separation.
4. Estimate fees, confirmation requirement, records, and rollback boundary.

### Build and prove

1. Create fresh destination keys when compromise or entropy is in question.
2. Record complete recovery and policy data.
3. Reconstruct from backup and compare several addresses.
4. Send a small test amount to the destination.
5. Spend part of that test amount back out, verifying change.

### Execute

1. Re-verify destination address(es) on signing devices.
2. Move a bounded first tranche unless an active theft race requires urgency.
3. Confirm and reconcile.
4. Move the remaining intended UTXOs with deliberate coin control and fees.
5. Update labels, cost/provenance records, inheritance, and inventory.

### Retire

Retain old recovery ability until every intended UTXO and change output is reconciled. Then wipe/reset obsolete devices appropriately, destroy unneeded temporary media, mark old backups as retired, and preserve only records required for history, tax, or incident evidence.

## Test programme

### Test 1 — Watch-only reconstruction

From descriptor/policy data, recreate wallet addresses and balances without private keys. Confirms wallet-definition backup and software interoperability.

### Test 2 — Key recovery

Using controlled spare hardware/offline tools, restore each intended key and verify fingerprint and derived addresses. Avoid bringing multiple multisig seeds into one general-purpose environment.

### Test 3 — End-to-end transaction

Receive a small amount, build a PSBT, sign using the normal paths, broadcast, confirm, and verify change and labels.

### Test 4 — One-component loss

Simulate one unavailable signer, destroyed backup, failed phone, lost laptop, unavailable cosigner, or unreachable location. Confirm the written response works.

### Test 5 — One-component compromise

Tabletop the response to a copied seed, stolen device, leaked xpub, or dishonest cosigner. Confirm a destination wallet and decision authority can be established without assembling all secrets unsafely.

### Test 6 — Incapacity/inheritance

With no secret disclosure beyond the design, have the intended executor/helper explain how they would authenticate the plan, locate components, obtain authority, reconstruct watch-only, and verify a beneficiary address.

## Change record template

For every material change record:

- date and reason;
- old and new architecture identifiers (never raw secrets);
- affected wallet layers and UTXOs;
- software/firmware versions;
- tests performed and outcomes;
- records/instructions updated;
- old components retired;
- unresolved risk and next review date.

## Signs the design is unhealthy

- The owner is afraid to transact because the process is too complex.
- Recovery depends on unrecorded memory.
- Nobody appropriate could recover after incapacity.
- Devices disagree on policy or addresses.
- Updates are avoided indefinitely because compatibility is unknown.
- Too many people/locations possess enough information to compromise funds.
- The architecture cannot be explained without revealing secrets.
- Small daily payments expose the deep reserve.
- Security work consumes more peace of mind and life energy than the risk warrants.

## Response to an advisory or alarming rumour

1. Do not enter seeds or install software from a link in a message/social post.
2. Identify affected product, model, version, feature, and key-generation date.
3. Confirm through authenticated project/vendor channels and independent technical analysis.
4. Determine whether the risk affects confidentiality, signing, entropy, privacy, or only availability.
5. Move to Amber while investigating; move to Red only when compromise or credible exposure warrants it.
6. If migration is required, build a known-good destination and act according to urgency.
7. Document the conclusion even if no action is taken.

## Validation tests

- All material wallets have an owner, purpose, architecture record, and review date.
- Software and firmware versions are known without exposing seeds.
- A migration can begin without searching social media for instructions.
- Tests cover both loss and compromise; these are opposite problems.
- Old wallet copies and instructions cannot silently remain authoritative.
- The process creates calm confidence rather than ritualised anxiety.

## Residual risks

Testing and updates introduce temporary exposure. Delayed updates can retain known vulnerabilities; rushed updates can introduce untested behaviour. Standards improve interoperability but implementations can contain bugs. Continuing competence and judgement remain necessary.
